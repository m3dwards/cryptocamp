Exercise 1 — DLEQ Sigma protocol (Chaum–Pedersen)

Goal: Public  (g, h, A, B) . Prover knows secret  a  with  A = gᵃ  and  B = hᵃ . Prove knowledge of that single  a .

Recall plain Schnorr (proves  A = gᵃ ): commit  R=gʳ , challenge  c , response  z=r+ca , check  gᶻ = R·Aᶜ .

The trick for DLEQ: use one random  r  but mirror every group-element step across both bases  g  and  h . One secret, one nonce, one challenge, one response — just checked twice.

Protocol:

1. Commit — Prover picks  r ← 𝔽  (i.e.  Z_q ) uniformly, sends
 R₁ = gʳ ,   R₂ = hʳ 
2. Challenge — Verifier sends random  c ← 𝔽 
3. Response — Prover sends  z = r + c·a   (mod  q )
4. Verify — Verifier accepts iff both:
 gᶻ = R₁ · Aᶜ   and   hᶻ = R₂ · Bᶜ 

Why it satisfies the three Sigma properties:

• Completeness:  gᶻ = g^{r+ca} = gʳ·(gᵃ)ᶜ = R₁·Aᶜ  ✓, and identically  hᶻ = hʳ·(hᵃ)ᶜ = R₂·Bᶜ  ✓.
• Special soundness: Two accepting transcripts with the same commitment but different challenges,  (R₁,R₂,c,z)  and  (R₁,R₂,c′,z′) ,  c≠c′ . From the  g -equation:  g^{z−z′} = A^{c−c′}  ⟹  a = (z−z′)/(c−c′) . The  h -equation gives the same value  (z−z′)/(c−c′) , so it's forced that  logₘ A = log_h B = a  — you extract the witness and it proves the two DLs are equal.
• Special HVZK: Simulator given  c : pick  z ← 𝔽  uniformly, set  R₁ = gᶻ·A^{−c} ,  R₂ = hᶻ·B^{−c} . Both equations hold by construction and  (R₁,R₂,c,z)  is distributed identically to a real transcript (real one is uniform in  z  too).

Setup: the "easy one way, hard the other way" trick

Pick a fixed number and call it  g  (the "base"). Everyone knows  g .

• Your secret is just a number. Call it  a .
• Your public key is  g  multiplied by itself  a  times. We write that  gᵃ  and call it  A .

So  A = gᵃ . (If  g=3  and  a=4 , then  A = 3×3×3×3 = 81 .)

The crucial fact: with huge numbers that "wrap around" (like a clock going past 12 back to 1), going forwards is easy — anyone can compute  A  from  a . But going backwards — figuring out  a  when you only see  A  — is practically impossible. That backwards problem is called the discrete log, and its hardness is the locked door the whole thing rests on.

So:  A  is safe to publish.  a  stays your secret. You want to prove "I know the  a  behind this  A " without revealing  a . That's exactly the job.

The two high-school rules we'll use

These are the only math tools needed. You've seen both:

1. Adding exponents:  g^(x+y) = gˣ · gʸ   (e.g.  2³⁺² = 2³ · 2² = 8×4 = 32 )
2. Multiplying exponents:  (gˣ)ʸ = g^(x·y)   (e.g.  (2³)² = 2⁶ = 64 )

That's it. Keep these two in your pocket.

Schnorr, as the three-step dance

Step 1 — Commit (the sealed envelope).
Prover picks a fresh random number  r , and sends  R = gʳ .
This is the envelope. Notice it's built the same way as the public key, but with the throwaway random  r  instead of the secret  a .

Step 2 — Challenge.
Verifier picks a random number  c  and sends it. Prover couldn't have known it in advance.

Step 3 — Response.
Prover computes one plain number:  z = r + c·a , and sends  z .

The check.
Verifier accepts if:   gᶻ  =  R · Aᶜ 

Why the check works (this is just the two rules)

Start from the left side,  gᶻ , and substitute  z = r + c·a :

•  gᶻ = g^(r + c·a) 
•  = gʳ · g^(c·a)   ← (rule 1: split the added exponents)
•  = gʳ · (gᵃ)ᶜ   ← (rule 2:  g^(c·a)  is  (gᵃ)  raised to  c )
•  = R · Aᶜ   ← (because  gʳ  is just  R , and  gᵃ  is just  A )

So an honest prover always passes. And every piece the verifier used —  R ,  c ,  A ,  z  — is public. The verifier never sees  a .

Why the secret stays hidden

Look at what the prover actually sent:  z = r + c·a . Because  r  was a fresh random number, it acts like a blindfold —  z  looks completely random and reveals nothing about  a . Send  A  and  R  and  z  all you like;  a  is smothered by  r .

Why a cheater can't fake it (and the "aha")

Suppose a faker somehow answered two different challenges,  c  and  c′ , for the same envelope  R . They'd have sent:

•  z  = r + c·a 
•  z′ = r + c′·a 

Subtract the second from the first — the  r  cancels:

•  z − z′ = (c − c′)·a 

Now just divide:   a = (z − z′) / (c − c′) 

That's high-school simultaneous equations — two equations, solve for the unknown. The point: anyone who can reliably answer two challenges about one envelope can have their secret pulled straight out. So being consistently convincing is knowing the secret. There's no cheat.

────────────────────

That's the entire Schnorr protocol. Commit an envelope  gʳ , get a random challenge  c , answer  r + c·a , and the check is just the two exponent rules clicking together.

And now DLEQ (Exercise 1) is a tiny step: instead of one public key  A = gᵃ , you have two —  A = gᵃ  and  B = hᵃ  — sharing the same secret  a  but different bases  g  and  h . So you run the exact same dance, but build two envelopes ( gʳ  and  hʳ ) with the same  r , and the verifier does the check twice. Same one secret, proven to sit behind both.

Let's do it. DLEQ, same slow pace.

First — why would anyone even want this?

You have one secret number  a . From it you made two public values, using two different bases:

•  A = gᵃ   (secret  a , base  g )
•  B = hᵃ   (the same secret  a , but base  h )

You want to prove: "the same hidden  a  sits behind both  A  and  B " — without revealing  a .

Why care? This shows up all over Bitcoin. For example, linking a value across two different settings so someone can verify "these two things really are tied to the same secret" — without leaking the secret itself. (Exercise 2 connects your answer to a real Bitcoin standard, BIP 374.) You don't need the applications to do the exercise, but it's nice to know it's not abstract busywork.

The name: DLEQ = Discrete Log EQuality. "Prove two discrete logs are equal." Pronounced "poodle" (PoDLE). Someone had fun naming it.

The insight in one line

Plain Schnorr proves "I know the  a  behind  A ."
DLEQ proves "I know an  a  behind  A , and it's the same  a  behind  B ."

You get there by running the Schnorr dance once, but mirroring every group step across both bases — using one random  r , one challenge  c , one response  z . One secret, one nonce, checked in two places. The "sameness" is forced precisely because you reused the same numbers on both sides.

The dance

Step 1 — Commit (two envelopes, one random number).
Prover picks one fresh random  r , and sends both:

•  R₁ = gʳ 
•  R₂ = hʳ 

Same  r  in both. That shared  r  is the glue.

Step 2 — Challenge.
Verifier picks one random  c  and sends it.

Step 3 — Response (one number).
Prover sends the single number:  z = r + c·a 
Exactly like Schnorr — there's only one secret and one nonce, so there's only one response.

The check (do it twice).
Verifier accepts only if both hold:

•  gᶻ = R₁ · Aᶜ 
•  hᶻ = R₂ · Bᶜ 

Why it works (same two rules, applied on each base)

Left check, using  z = r + c·a :

•  gᶻ = g^(r+c·a) = gʳ · (gᵃ)ᶜ = R₁ · Aᶜ  ✓

Right check, identical steps but with base  h :

•  hᶻ = h^(r+c·a) = hʳ · (hᵃ)ᶜ = R₂ · Bᶜ  ✓

Both are just rule 1 (split the added exponents) then rule 2 (pull out the  c ). An honest prover always passes both.

Why it proves the two secrets are equal (the new magic)

This is the only genuinely new bit versus Schnorr, and it's worth savouring.

Do the same "answer two challenges" trick as before. Suppose a prover answers two different challenges  c  and  c′  for the same pair of envelopes. From the  g -side check you extract, exactly like Schnorr:

•  a = (z − z′) / (c − c′) 

But from the  h -side check you can pull out a secret too — call it the number behind  B :

•  (that number) = (z − z′) / (c − c′) 

It's the same fraction. The  z s and  c s are shared, so both sides spit out the identical value. That's the proof: the discrete log of  A  (base  g ) and the discrete log of  B  (base  h ) are forced to be the same number. You can't pass unless they genuinely match. That's what "proves equality" means here.

Why the secret stays hidden

Same reason as Schnorr: the only response is  z = r + c·a , blindfolded by the random  r . And the two envelopes  gʳ ,  hʳ  leak nothing on their own. In fact, a verifier could fake a whole convincing transcript by themselves — pick  z  at random, then set  R₁ = gᶻ·A^(−c)  and  R₂ = hᶻ·B^(−c)  so the checks pass by construction. Since they can produce the transcript without the prover, watching a real one teaches them nothing about  a . That's the "zero-knowledge" property, concretely.

That is a complete answer to Exercise 1. In one breath: "Chaum–Pedersen protocol — commit  gʳ  and  hʳ  with the same  r , challenge  c , respond  z = r + c·a , verify both  gᶻ = R₁·Aᶜ  and  hᶻ = R₂·Bᶜ ." And you can now justify all three properties in plain words: honest provers pass (the two rules), cheaters get their secret extracted and it's forced equal on both sides (the shared fraction), and nothing leaks (the random  r  / self-fakeable transcript).
