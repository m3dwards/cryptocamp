What we're trying to show
There are two ways people define "E1 and E2 are independent":

The plain-English way: Pr[E1 | E2] = Pr[E1] — "learning E2 happened doesn't change how likely E1 is."
The textbook formula way: Pr[E1 ∩ E2] = Pr[E1] · Pr[E2] — "the chance of both happening equals the chance of E1 times the chance of E2."

Exercise 5 wants you to show these two are always the same thing — whenever one is true, the other is automatically true too. That's what "if and only if" means: it's a two-way street, so we need to check both directions separately.
A simple example to keep in your head
Flip two fair coins. E1 = "first coin is heads," E2 = "second coin is heads." Each has probability 1/2. These coins don't affect each other, so this should be a case of independence.
The one tool we need: the definition of conditional probability
Pr[E1 | E2] = Pr[E1 ∩ E2] / Pr[E2]
In plain words: "the chance of E1, given you know E2 happened" equals "the chance both happened" divided by "the chance E2 happened alone." This is just a definition — always true, whether or not E1 and E2 are independent.
Direction 1: Plain-English definition ⇒ Textbook formula
Start by assuming independence the plain-English way: Pr[E1 | E2] = Pr[E1].
In our coin example, that's saying Pr[E1 | E2] = 1/2.
Now swap in the definition of conditional probability for the left side:
Pr[E1 ∩ E2] / Pr[E2] = Pr[E1]
This is just an equation now — and we want to get rid of the division. So multiply both sides by Pr[E2]:
Pr[E1 ∩ E2] = Pr[E1] · Pr[E2]
And that's it — that's the textbook formula, derived. In numbers: Pr[E1 ∩ E2] = 1/2 · 1/2 = 1/4 (and indeed, "both coins heads" happens for exactly 1 out of 4 equally-likely outcomes — checks out).
Direction 2: Textbook formula ⇒ Plain-English definition
Now go backward. Start by assuming the textbook formula: Pr[E1 ∩ E2] = Pr[E1] · Pr[E2].
We want to get back to Pr[E1 | E2] = Pr[E1]. Looking at the conditional probability definition again — Pr[E1 | E2] = Pr[E1 ∩ E2] / Pr[E2] — notice the right side has Pr[E1 ∩ E2] in it. We know what that equals! Substitute it in:
Pr[E1 | E2] = (Pr[E1] · Pr[E2]) / Pr[E2]
Now Pr[E2] is just a number sitting on the top and the bottom — it cancels:
Pr[E1 | E2] = Pr[E1]
Done — that's the plain-English definition, recovered.
Why this counts as a full proof
Because we showed the implication goes both ways — definition 1 leads to definition 2 (Direction 1), and definition 2 leads back to definition 1 (Direction 2) — we've shown they're completely equivalent. Whichever one is true, the other is automatically true too. That's exactly what "if and only if" requires.
The only step doing any real work in either direction is "multiply both sides by Pr[E2]" or "cancel Pr[E2] from top and bottom" — same algebraic move, just run in opposite directions.
