AGM = Algebraic Group Model. It's a way of modeling adversaries that sits between two extremes, introduced by Fuchsbauer, Kiltz & Loss in 2018 (mentioned right in your Week 4 notes). It's the setting for Exercises 2 and 3.

The core idea

In a cryptographic group, the only "legitimate" operations are the group ones: multiplying elements, taking inverses, exponentiating (i.e., combining  g ,  pk , etc. with known exponents). The AGM formalizes an adversary that plays by these rules and nothing else.

The definition: an adversary is algebraic if, every time it outputs a group element  Z , it also outputs a list of exponents showing how  Z  was built from the group elements it has already seen (inputs + public parameters). If it was given  g, X, Y , then along with any  Z  it must output  (a, b, c)  such that:

Z = gᵃ · Xᵇ · Yᶜ

The intuition: the adversary can only produce new group elements by combining old ones, so it necessarily "knows" how it did so.

Why this is useful

It rules out "non-algebraic" adversaries — hypothetical attackers that somehow exploit the bit-level encoding of group elements or do something non-group-theoretic. Against an algebraic adversary, that representation list is a gift to the security proof: it hands you extra equations for free.

You saw the payoff directly in Exercise 3. Because the forger had to reveal how it built its group element  R :

R = g^{a₀} · pk^{a₁} · ∏ᵢ Rᵢ^{a_{i+1}}

that single equation, combined with the verification equation, gave you a linear equation in the secret key — solvable in one run, no rewinding. A normal (non-algebraic) proof needed rewinding/forking and lost an  ε²  factor.

The AGM is stronger/more realistic than the GGM (it doesn't hide the actual encoding of group elements — the adversary can look at them), but weaker than the standard model (it still restricts how the adversary produces elements).

The honest tradeoff (from your notes)

As the Week 4 notes stress: an AGM proof only shows there's no algebraic attack. A non-algebraic attacker might still exist in principle. So AGM security is a weaker guarantee than a standard-model proof — but in exchange you get:

• much cleaner proofs (no rewinding/forking),
• tight reductions (no squaring/forking-lemma loss),
• and nice facts like "in the AGM, DL ⇒ CDH" (Exercise 2) — many assumptions collapse to being equivalent to plain discrete log.

A key contrast worth remembering

The AGM is about how the adversary builds group elements (it reveals exponents). The ROM (Random Oracle Model) is about how the hash function behaves (modeled as a truly random function you can program). They're orthogonal, which is why the Schnorr proof in Exercise 3 uses both at once — AGM for the group element  R , ROM to program/simulate  H .
